## 1 · Substack post

**Title:** The five per cent

**Subtitle:** Almost every baby breathes on their own. The whole discipline exists for the ones who don't — and for the fact that you usually can't tell which.

---

Of a thousand babies born at term today, about eight hundred and fifty will breathe within thirty seconds. Another hundred will start after you dry them and rub their back. Around fifty will need someone to breathe for them. Fewer than ten will be intubated. One to three will receive chest compressions or adrenaline.

The number that should keep you up is the fifty.

Ventilation is common enough that everyone who attends a birth must be able to do it well, and uncommon enough that almost nobody gets enough practice to stay good at it. That is the gap. It is not a knowledge gap — most people can recite the algorithm. It is the gap between reciting it and doing it correctly, at speed, at two in the morning, on a baby who is not responding, with a bag you have not checked.

I have spent the last while building something to close a part of that gap.

It is a single HTML file. Twenty-four units. It works on a phone with the internet switched off. There is no account, no server, and no analytics — it collects nothing, which is both a privacy position and a practical one, because the labour rooms that need this most are the ones where the network drops.

Three things about it are deliberate and slightly unusual.

**There is no "mark as read" button.** Anywhere. A unit is mastered when you have answered both of its checkpoint questions correctly, and if you get one wrong later, the unit stops being mastered. Completion tracking measures exposure. It does not measure learning, and every figure downstream of it is corrupted by that.

**The certificate rests on three separate criteria**, not one score. Did you meet every unit? Did the learning survive a night's sleep — measured across the breadth of units, not by raw item count, because a raw count can be satisfied entirely from what you studied first? And can you integrate it, closed book? A single number conflates three different questions and answers none of them well.

**Every clinical panel is written twice.** Once for a unit with a blender, a T-piece and a cardiac monitor. Once for a district hospital with a self-inflating bag and a stethoscope. Not as an afterthought, not in smaller type — side by side, as equals. Guidance written for well-resourced settings gets taught unmodified in settings that do not have the equipment, and the quiet lesson that teaches is that correct practice is something that happens elsewhere.

There is one more thing in it that I went back and forth on, and then kept.

The last appendix publishes the module's own weaknesses. Twelve of them, in a list. The spaced-retrieval intervals are fixed rather than fitted to the individual. Two items per unit is thin sampling. The assessment is unproctored. The verification code on the certificate is re-derivable, not tamper-proof, and says so in print. Kirkpatrick levels three and four are entirely unmeasured — I have no evidence this changes what happens in a delivery room.

A module that claims to be evidence-governed and will not publish that list is not evidence-governed. It is marketing with references.

It is free, it is Creative Commons, and it is here: [link]

## 3 · WhatsApp / faculty group

Sharing a free module on neonatal resuscitation, written to the 2025 AHA/AAP and ILCOR guidelines.

24 units, ~30 hours. It's a single HTML file — download it once and it works on your phone with no internet, no login, no app. Nothing is tracked or sent anywhere.

It's mastery-based: you can't mark a unit read, you have to answer its two questions correctly, and Parts unlock in order. Hints are built in for when you're stuck. There's a certificate at the end based on three criteria, not one score.

Two things that may be useful for teaching: every clinical section is written for both a well-equipped unit *and* a district hospital with just a self-inflating bag; and the appendices include 12 OSCE stations, 4 simulation scenarios that run on a doll and printed vital-sign cards, and a faculty guide with standard-setting worksheets.

Not the NRP® and not affiliated with the AAP — an original module built on the published guidelines.
